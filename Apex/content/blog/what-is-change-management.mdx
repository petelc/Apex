Change management is one of those terms that gets thrown around constantly in IT circles — yet teams often struggle to define exactly what it means in practice. This guide cuts through the jargon and gives you a clear, actionable picture.

## The One-Sentence Definition

Change management is the process of ensuring that changes to your IT environment — whether to systems, applications, infrastructure, or processes — are planned, reviewed, approved, and documented in a controlled way.

That's it. Everything else is implementation detail.

## Why Bother?

The case for change management is simple: **uncontrolled change is the leading cause of IT outages**.

According to ITIL research, 80% of IT service outages are caused by changes to IT infrastructure or applications. Most of those outages are preventable with a structured process.

Beyond preventing outages, change management also:

- Creates an **audit trail** — essential for SOC2, ISO 27001, and regulatory compliance
- Reduces **mean time to recovery** when things do go wrong (you know exactly what changed)
- Builds **stakeholder confidence** — business leaders can trust that IT isn't flying blind
- Enables **faster, safer delivery** — counterintuitively, structure enables speed

## The Core Components

Every effective change management process has five elements:

### 1. Change Request
A formal record capturing what change is proposed, why, when, and what the rollback plan is. This is your paper trail.

### 2. Risk Assessment
Not all changes are equal. A typo fix in a blog post carries different risk than a database schema migration in production. Scoring changes by risk lets you apply appropriate oversight.

### 3. Approval Workflow
Who needs to sign off? For low-risk changes, maybe just a team lead. For high-risk changes touching production, you probably want a Change Advisory Board (CAB) review.

### 4. Scheduling
Changes should happen at controlled times — maintenance windows where the impact of a problem is minimised. Scheduling also prevents conflicting changes from running simultaneously.

### 5. Post-Implementation Review
Did the change succeed? Were there unintended consequences? This feedback loop is how your team improves over time.

## Change Types

ITIL 4 defines three change types that most organisations find useful:

| Type | Description | Typical Approval |
|------|-------------|-----------------|
| **Standard** | Pre-approved, low-risk, routine | Automated or team lead |
| **Normal** | Requires individual assessment | CAB review |
| **Emergency** | Urgent, breaks standard process | Emergency CAB or designated approver |

## Common Mistakes

**Treating change management as paperwork.** If your process creates friction without value, people route around it. The goal is visibility and control — not bureaucracy.

**CAB meetings that become rubber-stamping sessions.** If approvers aren't actually reviewing changes critically, your CAB is theatre. Keep CABs small, focused, and empowered to say no.

**No rollback plan.** Every change should have a documented rollback procedure. If you can't answer "how do we undo this?", the change isn't ready to go.

**Treating all changes the same.** A risk-based approach reserves heavy process for high-stakes changes and keeps standard changes moving fast.

## Getting Started

If you're starting from scratch, don't try to implement everything at once. Start with:

1. A simple change request form (title, description, risk, rollback plan)
2. A single approver for each change
3. A shared calendar for scheduled changes

That's a working change management process. Evolve from there.

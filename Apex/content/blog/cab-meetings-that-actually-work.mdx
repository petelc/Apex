Ask any IT manager about their CAB meetings and you'll get one of two responses: a sigh, or silence. The Change Advisory Board is one of the most theoretically sound — and practically broken — institutions in IT.

It doesn't have to be.

## The Problem With Most CABs

The typical CAB meeting looks like this: thirty minutes, twelve people, twenty-three changes on the agenda. Each change gets ninety seconds of review before being rubber-stamped through. Nobody has read the change request in advance. The CAB chair is doing the review live, in front of everyone, while silently praying nothing breaks over the weekend.

This isn't change management. It's change theatre.

The problems are structural:

- **Too many attendees.** More people means more scheduling overhead and less accountability. If everyone approves, nobody owns it.
- **No pre-reading.** Effective review requires time. Real review happens before the meeting, not during it.
- **Wrong cadence.** Weekly CABs create artificial backlogs. Low-risk changes queue behind high-risk ones for no reason.
- **No data.** Approvers are flying blind without historical context on change success rates and risk patterns.

## What Good Looks Like

Effective CABs share a few traits:

### Small, Accountable Membership
Three to five people who actually understand what they're approving. The goal is diverse perspective — business impact, technical risk, security — not representation.

### Async-First Review
Changes go into the queue 48 hours before the meeting. Reviewers read and comment before the room convenes. The meeting itself is for exceptions and decisions, not first-pass review.

### Risk-Based Scoping
Not every change needs CAB. Pre-approve standard changes (routine, low-risk, well-documented). CAB only reviews Normal changes that meet a risk threshold. Emergency changes get an expedited sub-group.

### Data in the Room
The best CABs review historical context alongside the change proposal: How many similar changes have failed? What's the submitter's track record? What's the current system health?

### Time-Boxed Agenda
Five minutes per change, hard stop. If a change can't be explained in five minutes, it's not ready to approve.

## A Practical CAB Format

Here's a format that works for most teams:

**Before the meeting (async):**
- Change submitters complete the template in full — no exceptions
- Reviewers read and leave comments in the system
- Risk scores are calculated automatically
- Any reviewer can flag a change for discussion

**In the meeting (30 minutes max):**
1. Flagged changes only — with pre-comments already visible (15 min)
2. Schedule conflicts and blackout window check (5 min)
3. Approvals — anything not flagged is approved in batch (5 min)
4. Action items (5 min)

**After the meeting:**
- Submitters get notified immediately
- Scheduled changes appear on the shared calendar
- CAB decisions are recorded in the audit trail

## The Emergency CAB

Every organisation needs a fast-track for genuine emergencies. Define it clearly:

- What qualifies? (Production outage, active security incident, regulatory breach)
- Who can declare an emergency? (Designated authority — not just anyone)
- Who approves? (Two specific named people, reachable 24/7)
- What's the retrospective process? (All emergency changes reviewed at the next regular CAB)

Keep the emergency bar high. If everything is an emergency, nothing is.

## Measuring CAB Effectiveness

If you're not measuring, you're guessing. Track:

- **Change success rate** — what % of approved changes succeed without incident?
- **CAB throughput** — how many changes reviewed per meeting?
- **Emergency change frequency** — trending up or down?
- **Time in queue** — how long does an average normal change wait for CAB?

Declining success rate after CAB approval is a signal your CAB isn't adding value. High time-in-queue is a signal of a bottleneck that's slowing the business.

## The Goal

The purpose of a CAB is to reduce change risk through diverse review and scheduling coordination — not to create a bureaucratic checkpoint. When your CAB is working, changes move faster (because risks are caught before they cause incidents) and your team trusts the process.

That's the version worth building.

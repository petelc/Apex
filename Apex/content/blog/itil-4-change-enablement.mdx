If you've been working in IT operations for more than a few years, you grew up with ITIL v3's Change Management practice. When ITIL 4 arrived, it renamed it to **Change Enablement** — and the name change wasn't cosmetic.

This post breaks down what's actually different, what stayed the same, and what it means for how your team operates day-to-day.

## What's the Same

Let's get this out of the way first: the core mechanics of change management haven't changed.

You still need:
- A change record for every significant change
- Risk and impact assessment
- An approval mechanism (still called CAB, though ITIL 4 encourages alternatives)
- A rollback plan
- Post-implementation review
- An audit trail

If your team already does these things well, ITIL 4 doesn't require you to redo anything.

## What Actually Changed

### The Philosophy: From Gatekeeping to Enabling

ITIL v3 was implicitly adversarial. "Change Management" was the control function that slowed things down to prevent incidents. The CAB was a gatekeeping body.

ITIL 4 explicitly reframes this. "Change Enablement" is a function that helps the organisation make changes **as fast as safely possible** — not as safely as possible regardless of speed. The emphasis on value co-creation and agility runs through everything.

In practice, this means your process should be optimised to reduce approval friction for low-risk changes, not to apply uniform process to everything.

### Three Change Types → More Explicit

ITIL 4 retained the three change type model but made the intent clearer:

**Standard changes:** Pre-authorised. Low risk, high frequency, well understood. These should have zero CAB involvement. If you're running every standard change through CAB, you're wasting everyone's time.

**Normal changes:** Need individual assessment. This is where CAB or equivalent review happens. ITIL 4 explicitly allows for virtual approval (async, tooling-based) rather than requiring a meeting.

**Emergency changes:** ITIL 4 puts more emphasis on post-implementation review for emergency changes and making them visible rather than hiding them. Every emergency change should be reviewed at the next CAB and tracked separately in your metrics.

### The Change Authority Model

ITIL v3 implied the CAB was the primary decision body. ITIL 4 introduces the concept of **Change Authority** more explicitly — the right person or group to approve a particular change can vary by change type, risk level, or organisational structure.

For your team, this means you should have defined change authorities for different risk bands:

| Risk Level | Change Authority |
|-----------|-----------------|
| Low (score 1–3) | Team lead or automated approval |
| Medium (score 4–6) | Designated change manager |
| High (score 7–9) | CAB review |
| Critical (score 10) | Senior leadership + CAB |

### Emphasis on Value Stream Thinking

ITIL 4 is built around value streams and service value chains. The implication for change enablement is that you should map the entire change lifecycle as a value stream and identify waste — steps that add delay without adding safety.

Common waste candidates:
- Manual copy-paste of information between systems
- Required sign-offs from people who never actually reject changes
- Waiting periods that aren't backed by testing or deployment logic

### Less Prescriptive About CAB

This is probably the most practically significant shift. ITIL 4 doesn't require a weekly CAB meeting. It requires an appropriate review mechanism for each change type. That could be:

- A Slack channel where designated reviewers approve changes
- An async review process in a tool (like APEX)
- A standing 15-minute daily meeting instead of a 60-minute weekly one
- AI-assisted risk scoring with auto-approval below a threshold

The constraint is effectiveness, not format.

## What ITIL 4 Doesn't Solve

ITIL 4 is a framework, not a recipe. It won't tell you:

- How long your change approval SLA should be
- How many people should be on your CAB
- What constitutes "high risk" in your environment
- How to handle the politics of getting departments to file change requests

These are your problems to solve. ITIL 4 gives you better language and conceptual tools to frame them — but the hard work of building a culture of change discipline is still yours.

## Practical Takeaways

If you're on ITIL v3 today and wondering whether to "upgrade," here's what I'd actually recommend:

1. **Stop calling it "Change Management."** Language shapes culture. "Change Enablement" signals to your team that the goal is enabling delivery, not blocking it.

2. **Review your standard change list.** When did you last update it? If standard changes still require CAB review, you've got dead weight in your process.

3. **Define change authorities explicitly.** Who approves what? Write it down. It should be in your tooling, not in someone's head.

4. **Add a post-implementation review habit for emergency changes.** This is the most commonly skipped ITIL 4 recommendation and the one with the highest value.

5. **Measure time-in-queue.** If normal changes are sitting in the queue for more than 48 hours, your approval mechanism has a problem.

None of this requires a certification or a consultancy engagement. It requires honest assessment and the will to simplify.
